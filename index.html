              Surviving a Midwest winter without water or power

              For a multi-day outage from an ice storm, blizzard, or grid failure. Set your household in the Planner and the amounts on the Checklist become yours.

              01Priorities: the rule of threes

              In a Midwest winter, the order of threats is different from what most people expect.

              You can survive roughlyWithoutWhat it means in winter
              3 hoursShelter and warmth in harsh conditionsHeat is priority one
              3 daysWaterWater is priority two
              3 weeksFoodFood matters, but less urgently
              The hidden killers: carbon monoxide poisoning, house fires from improvised heating, hypothermia, and falls on ice. Most outage deaths come from these, not from running out of food.

              02Heat & shelter

              Know what stops when the power goes out

              • Gas and propane furnaces need electricity for the blower and ignition, so they stop in an outage. So do most boilers and heat pumps.
              • A generator can run a furnace only through a transfer switch or inlet installed by an electrician.
              • Gas fireplaces and gas logs may light by hand if they don't need power; check the manual. Vent-free gas logs need ventilation and a CO detector.

              Create a warm room

              • Pick a room with few windows and exterior walls, ideally on the lowest above-ground floor.
              • Allow about 20 square feet per sleeping person, and keep 3 feet clear around the heater.
              • Close off every other room. Lay towels or blankets along door gaps. Cover windows, but keep one you can crack for ventilation.
              • Pitch a tent indoors. A camping tent full of people traps body heat remarkably well.
              • Put rugs, foam mats, or blankets on the floor. Cold floors pull heat out fast.

              Safe heat sources

              SourceIndoors?Notes
              Portable propane heater labeled for indoor usewith precautionsLook for low-oxygen and tip-over shutoffs. Ventilate as the manual directs and run a CO detector. Don't run it while everyone sleeps unless the manual allows it.
              Wood stove or fireplace insertyesProperly installed with a clean chimney. Open fireplaces lose most heat up the flue.
              Kerosene heater labeled for indoor usecautionNeeds ventilation and gives off fumes. Use 1-K kerosene only. Some places restrict them.
              Body heat, hot water bottles, hand warmersyesUnderrated. Wrap hot water bottles so they can't burn skin.

              Carbon monoxide and fire

              • Put a battery-powered CO detector in the warm room and every sleeping area.
              • Symptoms: headache, dizziness, nausea, weakness, confusion, sleepiness. If several people feel flu-like at once, suspect CO. Get everyone outside and call 911.
              • Keep heaters 3 feet from bedding and furniture, keep an ABC extinguisher in the warm room, and use LED lights instead of candles.

              03Water

              • At least 1 gallon per person per day for drinking and basic sanitation. Children, nursing mothers, and sick people may need more.
              • On a private well, the pump stops when the power does. Fill every container before the storm.
              • Store water where it won't freeze, or leave headspace in containers. Replace self-filled water every 6 months.
              • Water heater: 30–80 gallons. Shut off the power or gas and the cold inlet, then drain from the bottom valve. Refill before turning it back on.
              • Bathtub: fill it before the storm. Use it for flushing and washing, or treat it before drinking.

              Treating snow, ice, and questionable water

              • Boil: a rolling boil for 1 minute, or 3 minutes above 5,000 feet.
              • Bleach: unscented household bleach. 8 drops per gallon for 6%, 6 drops for 8.25%. Stir and wait 30 minutes.
              • Cloudy water: let it settle and pour it through a clean cloth or coffee filter first.
              • Snow: about 10 inches makes 1 inch of water and burns a lot of fuel to melt. Treat it before drinking, and never eat snow directly.

              04Food & cooking

              Stock food that needs no refrigeration, little water, and little or no cooking. Staying warm burns energy, so the Planner adds a cold-weather margin.

              • Ready-to-eat: canned meats and fish, peanut butter, crackers, bars, trail mix, dried fruit, canned fruit and vegetables.
              • Hot food helps morale: soups, chili, oats, cocoa. Cook outdoors on camp stoves and grills, or indoors only on a stove labeled for indoor use.
              • Many gas ranges can be lit with a match during an outage. Check your manual, ventilate, and use it only for cooking, never for heat.
              • Keep a manual can opener. It's the item people forget most.
              • A full freezer holds about 48 hours, a half-full one about 24, and a fridge about 4. Throw out perishables above 40°F for more than 2 hours.

              05Clothing & sleep

              • Layer: wool or synthetic base, fleece or wool middle, wind- and water-resistant shell. No cotton; it holds moisture.
              • Wear a hat indoors, keep socks dry, and choose mittens over gloves.
              • A sleeping bag rated 0°F or lower for everyone over age 1, with a pad underneath. Insulation under you matters more than on top.
              • Eat a fatty snack before bed. Sleep together in the warm room or the indoor tent.

              06Light, power & communication

              • A headlamp per person, LED lanterns, and spare batteries kept in the warm room. Avoid candles.
              • Power banks charge a phone 3–4 times each. A portable power station runs lights, phones, and a CPAP; the Planner sizes one.
              • Put phones in low-power mode and send texts instead of calling; texts often get through when calls don't.
              • A hand-crank or battery NOAA weather radio works when cell service doesn't.
              Generators run outdoors only, more than 20 feet from windows, doors, and vents. Never in a garage or on a porch. Use a transfer switch installed by an electrician, or outdoor-rated cords. Never backfeed house wiring.

              07Sanitation & hygiene

              • With stored water, flush by pouring about 1–1.5 gallons straight into the bowl.
              • Otherwise make a bucket toilet: a 5-gallon bucket, seat lid, heavy contractor bags, and kitty litter or sawdust. Seal bags and store them outside.
              • Hand sanitizer (60%+ alcohol) and wipes. Paper plates so you don't wash dishes. Keep trash sealed and outside.

              08Health, first aid & safety

              HypothermiaSignsAction
              mildShivering, numb hands, clumsinessWarm, dry clothes; warm (not hot) drinks; food
              moderateViolent shivering, confusion, slurred speechRewarm the core (chest, neck, groin). Get medical help.
              severeShivering stops, drowsiness, weak pulseCall 911. Handle gently. Don't rub limbs.
              • Frostbite: white or grayish skin, waxy feel, numbness. Warm with body heat or warm (not hot) water. Don't rub it, and don't rewarm if it could refreeze.
              • Keep a 2+ week supply of prescriptions. Keep refrigerated medicine cool but never frozen.
              • Shoveling can trigger heart attacks; go slow and push rather than lift. Stay well back from downed lines (at least 35 feet).

              09Pet survival

              Pets chill faster than you think and depend on you for every need. Plan for them the same way you plan for people.

              DOG

              • Food and water for the whole outage; about 1 oz of water per pound per day.
              • Medication and vet records in a waterproof bag.
              • Warmth: bring them into the warm room; a coat for short-haired dogs.
              • ID tag and microchip up to date.
              • Leash and short bathroom trips; wipe paws after ice melt.
              • Crate or carrier in case you evacuate.

              CAT

              • Food and water; cats need about 4 oz of water a day.
              • Medication and vet records.
              • Warmth: a covered bed or box in the warm room.
              • ID collar and microchip.
              • Litter and a disposable box.
              • Carrier with a towel, ready by the door.

              Many warming centers don't accept pets. Find pet-friendly options before winter.

              10Personal protection

              Outages bring out good neighbors and, sometimes, opportunists. Most of protecting yourself is low-key: secure the house, verify who's at the door, and look out for each other.

              Home security in an outage

              • Lock doors and windows even while you're home. Put a bar or dowel in sliding-door tracks.
              • Keep entrances lit with solar or battery motion lights.
              • Keep supplies, fuel, and generators out of view. Generators are a common theft target; lock or chain yours in place outdoors.
              • Don't post that you're away, or what supplies you have.

              Someone at the door

              • Don't open the door to unexpected visitors. Talk through the door or a window.
              • People claiming to be from the utility: call the number on your bill to check. Scammers pose as utility workers and demand immediate payment, often by gift card, wire, or crypto, to avoid a shutoff.
              • Repair offers after the storm: get written estimates, check licenses, and don't pay large amounts up front.

              Personal safety

              • Go out in pairs and tell someone where you're going and when you'll be back.
              • Avoid confrontations. Property can be replaced. If you feel threatened, get to safety and call 911.
              • If someone lives alone, set a daily check-in with a neighbor or relative.

              Protective gear

              • N95 masks for wood smoke, ash, dust, or if someone gets sick.
              • Work gloves and safety glasses for chopping wood, clearing ice, and handling debris.
              • Ice cleats for walking outside.

              Firearms in the home

              • With everyone sharing one warm room, and especially with children present, keep firearms unloaded and locked, with ammunition locked separately, and keys or codes out of reach.
              • Follow federal, state, and local law.

              11Special situations

              Infants and young children

              • Babies lose heat fast. Check the back of the neck or the chest, not the hands.
              • Safe sleep still applies: on their back, in their own crib or bassinet, in a sleep sack, with no loose blankets or adult sleeping bags.
              • Ready-to-feed formula needs no water. Skip whole nuts, popcorn, and spoonfuls of peanut butter for children under 4.

              Older adults and powered medical equipment

              • Older adults chill faster and may not notice. Check on them often and move them to a warming center early.
              • Ask your utility about a medical priority list for customers who rely on powered equipment.
              • Home oxygen: a power station can't run most concentrators for long. Arrange backup cylinders with your supplier now.
              • CPAP: ask about a DC cord or battery; running without the humidifier uses far less power.

              Apartments and mobile homes

              • Many leases and fire codes restrict propane cylinders and kerosene in apartments. Check before you buy a heater.
              • Mobile homes lose heat fast and have exposed pipes. Plan to leave earlier.

              12Protecting your home

              • Short loss of heat: let faucets on exterior walls drip, open sink cabinets, and insulate exposed pipes.
              • Long loss of heat: shut off the main water valve and open every faucet, top floor first. Flush toilets to empty tanks. Pour non-toxic RV antifreeze into traps.
              • Disconnect hoses and cover outdoor faucets before a freeze.
              • Find your water shutoff, gas shutoff, and breaker panel now, and write them on the Sheet.
              • Unplug sensitive electronics so the surge when power returns doesn't damage them.

              13Vehicle & stranded

              Keep the tank at least half full all winter, and keep a winter kit in every car.

              DO

              • Stay with the vehicle unless help is visible and close
              • Clear snow from the tailpipe before running the engine
              • Run the engine about 10 minutes an hour for heat, with a window cracked
              • Turn on the dome light or hazards while the engine runs
              • Tie a bright cloth to the antenna
              • Move your arms and legs to keep blood flowing

              DON'T

              • Run the engine with the tailpipe blocked
              • Run the engine continuously
              • Wander off in blowing snow
              • Drain the battery with lights and chargers
              • Eat snow

              ··Worst case

              These scenarios are unlikely. Planning for them mostly means doing the ordinary things for longer, and leaning on your neighbors and community.

              Grid down for weeks to months

              • Water: storage plus a way to make more. Keep spare filter cartridges, collect and treat snow and rain, and know where public water distribution would be.
              • Sanitation: this becomes the biggest health risk. Bag-and-bucket toilets, sealed waste stored away from living space and water sources, and handwashing every time.
              • Heat: households can combine into one heated home to share fuel and body heat. Plan with neighbors or family now.
              • Food: 30–90 days of staples you already eat: rice, beans, oats, canned goods, and a safe way to cook them.
              • Medical: ask your doctor whether you can keep an extended supply of essential medications. Take a first-aid course.
              • Communication: FRS walkie-talkies need no license. GMRS and amateur (ham) radio need FCC licenses but reach farther.
              • Community: organize neighbors for check-ins, shared watch, and pooled resources. Communities that cooperate do far better than lone households.
              • Mental health: keep routines, get daylight, sleep, and give everyone a job.
              • Relocation: decide in advance what would make you leave, and where you'd go.

              Radiological emergency

              • Get inside, stay inside, stay tuned. Go to an interior room or basement away from windows and doors.
              • Stay inside at least 24 hours unless officials tell you otherwise.
              • Close windows, doors, and the fireplace damper. Turn off fans and forced-air heat that pull in outside air.
              • If you were outside, remove your outer clothing and wash your skin with soap and water. Wash pets with gloves and a mask on.
              • Sealed food and drinks are safe. Boiling water does not remove radioactive material.
              • Winter caution: fuel-burning heaters need ventilation. If you've sealed the room, don't run them; rely on layers, sleeping bags, and body heat, and follow officials on when to ventilate.

              Illness outbreak in winter

              • Set aside a separate sick room if you can, and keep sick people away from infants and older adults.
              • Masks, handwashing, and cleaning shared surfaces.
              • Follow public health officials for testing, treatment, and isolation guidance.

                About WinterReady

                A small planner for getting a household through a winter storm and outage.

                Ice storms and blizzards take out power and water across the Midwest most winters. The advice for getting through one is well known but scattered. WinterReady turns it into a status you can read at a glance, amounts sized to your household, steps for the situation you're in, and a job for everyone in the house.

                It's small on purpose. There's no account and no server. Everything you enter stays in this browser.

                How to use it

                1. Fill in the Planner: who lives there, your home, and what you already have.
                2. Shop from the Checklist until your readiness is where you want it.
                3. Give everyone a job in Family, and print the Sheet.
                4. When a storm is coming, raise the severity in the header. Status shows your next actions and Tonight's plan.
                5. In an emergency, press Emergency.

                How the advice is made

                Tonight's plan and “What you can do” come from fixed rules applied to your profile and conditions, not from AI. Every step shows why it's there and links to its source, so you can check it.
